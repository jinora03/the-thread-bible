# Scripture & Content Philosophy

Every file in `content/` is written to these guidelines. Part 1 is the editorial philosophy. Part 2 explains how it maps onto the content files, plus the house style that `npm run content:check` enforces.

---

## Part 1: Philosophy

The Thread Bible is primarily a **Scripture guide**, not a theological debate platform.

Its purpose is to help readers discover how the Bible connects to itself through:

- cross-references
- repeated themes
- prophecy and fulfillment
- promise and fulfillment
- type and antitype
- quotations and allusions
- covenants
- biblical events
- people and genealogies
- symbols
- doctrines explicitly grounded in Scripture
- Old Testament and New Testament connections
- the unfolding biblical story centered ultimately on Christ

The experience should resemble the philosophy of a high-quality **chain-reference Bible or study Bible** more than an academic theology textbook.

The primary rule is:

> **Let Scripture explain Scripture.**

### General theological direction

Use a broadly historic, orthodox, evangelical and Baptist-leaning understanding of Scripture, while avoiding unnecessary denominational language.

The content should generally reflect these convictions:

- Scripture is the primary and final authority for biblical teaching.
- God is the Creator and sovereign Lord.
- Jesus Christ is the center and fulfillment of the biblical story.
- Jesus Christ is fully divine and truly human.
- Christ's death and resurrection are central to salvation.
- Salvation is by God's grace through faith in Jesus Christ, grounded in Christ's finished work rather than human merit.
- Good works, obedience and a transformed life flow from salvation rather than earning salvation.
- Scripture should be read in its literary, historical and biblical context.
- The Old and New Testaments form one connected redemptive story.
- Prophecy, covenants, promises, patterns and biblical themes frequently develop across multiple books before reaching later fulfillment.
- Jesus' own teaching should be taken seriously, including costly discipleship, humility, mercy, holiness, forgiveness, love of enemies, care for others and obedience to God.
- Human traditions, institutions and theological systems should never be allowed to overshadow clear biblical teaching.

Do not constantly identify these positions as "Baptist teaching." The reader should primarily encounter **the Bible**, not denominational branding.

### Do not turn every passage into a theological debate

Avoid repeatedly writing things like:

> "Christians differ on how this passage should be interpreted..."

> "Some Christians believe X, while others believe Y..."

> "There are three major theological positions..."

That style should not be the default. It distracts from the purpose of the application and unnecessarily introduces debates into passages where they are not needed.

The default approach should instead be:

1. What does the passage actually say?
2. What is its immediate context?
3. What other Scriptures illuminate it?
4. Where does this theme appear elsewhere?
5. Does the New Testament quote or reference it?
6. Does it connect to Christ, covenant, promise, prophecy, doctrine or another biblical theme?
7. What concise explanation helps the reader understand the connection?

Only introduce competing interpretations when a disagreement materially changes the meaning of the passage and ignoring it would mislead the reader. Even then, keep discussion concise, neutral and secondary.

The app should never feel like a catalog of denominational disagreements.

#### Example

Avoid this style:

> "Christians differ on how the days should be read, as ordinary days, a literary framework, or long ages, while sharing the chapter's central claim that God alone is Creator."

Prefer something like:

> **Genesis 1 presents God as the Creator of all things. Creation comes into being by His command, is ordered according to His purpose, and is repeatedly declared good. The rest of Scripture returns to this foundation when describing God's authority over creation and Christ's role in creation.**

Then provide references:

**Related Scripture:** Psalm 33:6–9 · Isaiah 40:26 · John 1:1–3 · Colossians 1:16–17 · Hebrews 11:3 · Revelation 4:11

The application does not need to settle the age-of-the-earth question while explaining Genesis 1 unless the user specifically opens a study dealing with that question.

### Scripture first

Scripture should always have greater authority and visual prominence than commentary. A content unit should generally follow this hierarchy:

1. **Scripture:** the biblical text or passage reference.
2. **Connections:** other passages directly related to it.
3. **Context:** a concise explanation of historical, literary or narrative context.
4. **Insight:** a short explanation of why the connection matters.
5. **Further Study:** additional passages or topics the reader can explore.

Commentary should normally be short. Do not write five paragraphs where two sentences and six Scripture references would accomplish the purpose better.

### Cross-references are central

Cross-referencing is one of the most important functions of the application. Whenever possible, show the reader the biblical trail. For example, **the Passover Lamb:**

Exodus 12 → Isaiah 53:7 → John 1:29 → John 19:14–36 → 1 Corinthians 5:7 → 1 Peter 1:18–19 → Revelation 5

Provide a brief explanation of the connection, but allow the passages themselves to carry most of the weight.

### Christ-centered without forcing Christ into every verse

The biblical story ultimately points toward and culminates in Jesus Christ. Important connections include messianic prophecy, promise and fulfillment, covenant development, sacrifice, priesthood, kingship, kingdom, temple, Passover, the seed promise, the Son of David, the suffering servant, resurrection, firstfruits, Adam and Christ, and creation and new creation.

However, do not manufacture Christological connections where Scripture does not reasonably support them. Do not turn every rock, tree, number or minor narrative detail into a symbol of Christ.

Prefer connections that are:

1. explicitly identified by Scripture;
2. strongly supported by multiple passages; or
3. clearly part of a recurring biblical theme.

The goal is to reveal genuine biblical connections, not clever speculation.

### Prophecy

For prophecy pages, prioritize:

1. the original passage
2. historical context
3. important wording
4. related Old Testament passages
5. New Testament quotation or allusion
6. fulfillment where Scripture identifies it
7. related themes

For example, for Isaiah 53: brief original context; the key passage (Isaiah 53:4–6); the New Testament connections (Matthew 8:17, Luke 22:37, Acts 8:30–35, 1 Peter 2:22–25); then a brief explanation of how the New Testament writers use Isaiah's language. This is preferable to immediately presenting competing theological schools.

### Typology

Treat typology carefully. When the New Testament itself establishes the relationship, present it confidently. Examples include Adam and Christ, Passover, wilderness, manna, the bronze serpent, priesthood, sacrifices, temple, Jonah and firstfruits.

When a connection is suggestive rather than explicitly stated, describe it more cautiously and let the references show why the relationship is being presented. Avoid speculative typology.

### Doctrine

Doctrine pages should not begin with denominational formulations. Begin with Scripture. For example, **Salvation by Grace:**

- **Key Scriptures:** Ephesians 2:8–10 · Romans 3:21–28 · Romans 5:1 · Titus 3:4–7 · John 3:16 · Acts 16:30–31
- **Summary:** Salvation originates in God's grace and rests upon the saving work of Jesus Christ. It is received through faith rather than earned through human works. The life that follows salvation is therefore one of good works prepared for the believer to walk in.

Then allow readers to follow the references themselves. The explanation should summarize Scripture rather than replace it.

### Grace, faith and works

When passages concerning salvation appear, maintain this basic distinction:

- **Christ's work is the basis of salvation.**
- **Grace is its source.**
- **Faith receives what God provides.**
- **Good works are the resulting life of the believer, not the price paid for salvation.**

Where relevant, connect passages such as Ephesians 2:8–10, Romans 3:24–28, Romans 4, James 2, Titus 3:5–8 and John 15. Do not create false opposition between passages. Show how the passages relate to one another.

### Jesus' teachings

Do not soften difficult teachings merely because they are demanding. When Jesus teaches love your enemies, forgive others, deny yourself, take up your cross, serve rather than dominate, care for the poor and vulnerable, reject hypocrisy, pursue holiness, show mercy, speak truth, and trust God rather than wealth or status, present those teachings plainly.

Do not immediately explain them away. First allow the reader to encounter the force of Jesus' words. Then provide context and related passages. The app should take Jesus seriously even when His teaching challenges comfortable cultural or religious assumptions.

### Mainstream vs. fringe interpretations

Do not use the phrase "mainstream belief" as a constant authority. Instead prioritize interpretations that have strong support from:

- the immediate biblical context
- explicit cross-references
- New Testament use of Old Testament passages
- the broader canonical context
- widely recognized historic Christian teaching

Do not elevate unusual or highly speculative interpretations to equal prominence merely because someone holds them. A fringe theory does not automatically deserve equal space beside a strongly established reading. At the same time, do not pretend Scripture explicitly says something when it does not.

### Fact, Scripture and explanation

Maintain a clear internal distinction among:

- **Biblical text:** what Scripture actually says.
- **Biblical connection:** a relationship demonstrable from other passages.
- **Historical context:** relevant historical, geographical, linguistic or cultural information.
- **Explanation:** a concise explanation intended to help the reader understand the passages.
- **Interpretation:** a conclusion that goes beyond what is directly stated.

The farther content moves from explicit Scripture, the more restrained its wording should become.

### Writing style

Write in a tone that is clear, reverent, confident where Scripture is clear, restrained where Scripture is less explicit, concise, understandable, thoughtful, non-combative, Christ-centered and Scripture-saturated.

Avoid academic jargon where plain English works, excessive theological terminology, denominational arguments, sermonizing, emotional manipulation, speculative claims, unnecessary apologetic arguments, constantly telling users what "different Christians believe", and presenting commentary as though it were inspired Scripture.

The reader should frequently finish an explanation thinking *"I understand why those passages connect"*, rather than *"The app just told me which theological camp to join."*

### The Thompson chain-reference principle

Use this as a major inspiration: **passage → theme → related passage → related theme → broader biblical story.**

A reader should be able to begin with Genesis 3:15 and naturally discover: the seed → the Abrahamic promise → Judah → David → messianic prophecy → Christ → the cross → the resurrection → the new creation.

The application should function like an interconnected map of Scripture. Commentary exists primarily to help the reader follow the thread.

### Final content rule

- Between **more commentary** and **better Scripture connections**, prefer the Scripture connections.
- Between **introducing a theological controversy** and **helping the reader understand the passage in context**, prefer understanding the passage.
- Whenever Scripture clearly establishes a connection, show it plainly.
- Whenever the connection is uncertain, remain modest.

The purpose of The Thread Bible is not to make the reader dependent on the application's opinions. It is to help the reader become increasingly capable of seeing **the threads already present throughout Scripture**.

## Part 2: Applying it to the content files

### Doctrine pages (`doctrines/*.yaml`, `article:`)

| Field | Section | Guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `keyScriptures` | Key Scriptures | The passages the teaching rests on, shown in full. Three to six. |
| `teaching` | What Scripture Teaches | One or two short paragraphs that summarize the passages. Not a replacement for them. |
| `foundation.oldTestament` / `.newTestament` | Through Scripture | Where the teaching appears, written as a trail of references with a sentence each. |
| `insights` | Why It Matters | Two to four points, one or two sentences each, each anchored in a reference. |
| `questions` | Questions | Optional. Only questions Scripture itself raises or answers (e.g. 1 Corinthians 15:35). Not apologetics. |
| `interpretationNote` | A note on interpretation | Optional and rare. See below. |
| `furtherStudyIds` | Further Study | Related entries to explore next. |

### Prophecy pages (`prophecies/*.yaml`, `detail:`)

| Field | Section | Guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `passage` (top level) | The Passage | Shown first, in full. |
| `historicalContext` | Original Context | Brief: setting and audience. Leave authorship and dating debates out. |
| `keyWording` | Key Wording | Important phrases: `label`, `reference`, and a one-sentence `note`. |
| `oldTestament` | Related Old Testament Passages | References only. |
| `connectionsIntroduction` | In the New Testament | One or two sentences on how the New Testament writers use the passage. The rows come from `connections/`. |
| `themeIds` | Themes | |
| `interpretationNote` | A note on interpretation | Optional and rare. |

### Chapter notes (`chapter-notes/*.yaml`)

- `overview`: two to four sentences on what the chapter presents and where Scripture returns to it. Follow the Genesis 1 example above.
- `relatedScripture`: four to eight passages that illuminate the chapter as a whole.
- `crossReferences`: verse-by-verse references.

### Connections (`connections/*.yaml`)

Each connection's `basis` says how firmly it is grounded, and the reader sees it as a label:

| `basis` | Label | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `stated` | Stated in Scripture | Scripture quotes or explicitly identifies the link. |
| `supported` | Supported across Scripture | A clear verbal echo or recurring pattern that several passages support. |
| `suggested` | Suggested connection | Suggestive rather than established. Describe it cautiously, or leave it out if it is speculative. |

`description` is a short, neutral phrase naming what links the passages.

### Threads (`threads/*.yaml`)

A thread is a chain-reference trail: an ordered list of `steps`, each a `reference`, a short `title` and a `label` for its relation to the story (for example Promise, Type or Fulfillment). Any entry can follow one with `threadId: <file name>`, and the thread appears on its page under "Follow the thread".

### When to write an `interpretationNote`

Only when a disagreement materially changes what the passage means, and leaving it out would mislead the reader. Write two or three neutral sentences. Do not list positions by denomination, and do not use it for questions the passage is not asking (such as the age of the earth in Genesis 1).

### House style

- **No long dash (the em dash).** Use a comma, colon, parentheses or a full stop instead. `npm run content:check` rejects it.
- The en dash (–) is used only in Scripture ranges: `Isaiah 53:4–6`, `Isaiah 52:13–53:12`.
- Capitalize pronouns that refer to God: He, His, Him.
- **Refer to Scripture; don't paste it.** The text is shown in whichever translation the reader chooses. A short phrase may be quoted as a label, but references carry the weight.
- Link with tokens: `[[John 1:29]]`, `[[John 1:29|the Lamb of God]]`, `[[theme:covenant]]`.
- Plain English. Short sentences. No sermonizing.
